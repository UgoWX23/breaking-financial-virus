<p class="lead">The Covid crash is a rare natural experiment: a shock that came from outside the financial system, with a known calendar, and that spread across countries in a few weeks.</p>

<ul class="timeline">
  <li><time>31 Dec 2019</time>China reports a cluster of pneumonia cases in Wuhan to the WHO.</li>
  <li><time>21 Jan 2020</time>First confirmed case in the United States.</li>
  <li><time>30 Jan 2020</time>The WHO declares a public health emergency of international concern. Markets barely react.</li>
  <li><time>19 Feb 2020</time>The S&amp;P 500 reaches its peak. This is where our crisis window starts.</li>
  <li><time>21-24 Feb 2020</time>Outbreak in northern Italy. On 24 February, US stocks have their first sharp fall.</li>
  <li><time>9-16 Mar 2020</time>Several trading halts. The VIX, the market's fear index, reaches its highest level since 2008.</li>
  <li><time>11 Mar 2020</time>The WHO calls Covid-19 a pandemic.</li>
  <li><time>23 Mar 2020</time>The S&amp;P 500 hits its low, about a third below its peak. This is where our window ends.</li>
  <li><time>Late Mar 2020</time>Central banks and governments announce large support measures. Markets start to recover.</li>
</ul>

## Why Covid is the best case for our question

- **Speed.** Our Covid window lasts 24 trading days, against 356 for 2008 and 648 for the dot-com. A fast crisis makes propagation visible day by day.
- **Almost complete data.** Nearly every security listed in 2020 is in our data, so survivorship bias is minimal. For earlier crises we only see the survivors.
- **An external timeline.** The spread of the virus is documented by country, which lets us compare it with the spread of the crash across regions.

We keep 2008 and the dot-com in the background, as robustness checks and for the methodology, not as part of the story.
